Bringing 1907 Back to Life

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection
By Sherman Chan

The Magic of Early Photography

Imagine it's 1907. Color photography hasn't really been invented yet, but Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii has a brilliant idea: what if he takes three black-and-white photos of the exact same scene, but puts a Blue, Green, and Red glass filter over his lens for each one?

He captured all three of these exposures onto one single, tall, grey glass plate (like the one shown here). From top to bottom, they represent the Blue, Green, and Red color channels.

If you project those three photos on top of each other using colored lights, you get a full-color image! The only problem? Because he took the photos one after another, the camera (or the people in the photo) shifted slightly between shots. If we just stack them directly, the result is a blurry, misaligned mess.

In this project, I wrote a Python algorithm to automatically slice these glass plates into thirds and slide the separate color channels around until they perfectly align, bringing these 100-year-old photos back to life.

The raw glass plate (Top: Blue, Middle: Green, Bottom: Red)

Step 1: The Brute Force Approach

For the smaller, low-resolution JPEG images, the solution is fairly simple: Exhaustive Search. I sliced the raw glass plate image into three horizontal pieces (Blue, Green, Red). Keeping the Blue channel stationary, the algorithm takes the Green channel and tests shifting it in every single direction within a [-15, 15] pixel window.

To grade how well a shift aligns, I used the Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC) metric. Unlike a simple subtraction (L2 norm), NCC normalizes the brightness of the images, meaning a naturally dark red channel can still mathematically match a bright blue channel. Whichever shift produces the highest NCC score wins!

Step 2: When Things Get Too Big

While exhaustive search works great for small images, the high-resolution TIFF files are massive—often over 3,000 pixels wide. Searching over a massive 150-pixel radius pixel-by-pixel would take hours per image.

To solve this, I built an Image Pyramid. The algorithm shrinks the massive image in half recursively until it is just a tiny thumbnail. It runs the exhaustive search on the thumbnail (which takes milliseconds), finds a rough alignment, and then scales that guess back up to the larger image, doing a tiny [-2, 2] pixel search to refine it. This "coarse-to-fine" strategy aligned 70MB files in just seconds!

The Elephant in the Room: The Emir

Almost every basic algorithm fails on one specific image: The Emir of Bukhara. If you use standard pixel-matching, you get the blurry, misaligned mess on the left. Why?

Because the Emir's blue robes are incredibly bright in the Blue filter, but pitch black in the Red filter! The algorithm gets confused trying to match white pixels to black pixels.

Before (Failed Vanilla Alignment)

The algorithm fails to match the brightness differences.

After (Edge-Based Alignment)

Perfectly aligned using Sobel edge detection!

The Fix (Bells & Whistles): Instead of aligning raw colors, I ran the images through a Sobel Filter to extract the edges. Even if the robes change color, the outline of the robes remains identical across all channels. By aligning the edges instead of the colors, my algorithm successfully aligned the Emir (and every other image) perfectly!

Sprinkling Some Magic (More Bells & Whistles)

Just aligning the images wasn't enough—I wanted them to look like professional restorations. Notice how vibrant the "After" image of the Emir looks above compared to the dull "Before" image? I implemented three automatic post-processing tricks:

  • Automatic Cropping: The glass plates have messy borders that create ugly colored banding. The algorithm detects and trims 5% off the edges for a clean frame.
  • Automatic Contrasting: Old photos look washed out. The algorithm finds the darkest 1% of pixels and forces them to pure black, and the brightest 1% to pure white, dynamically stretching the contrast.
  • Automatic White Balance: To remove the old yellow tinting, I used the Gray World Assumption. By mathematically forcing the average brightness of the Red, Green, and Blue channels to be equal, the colors immediately look natural to the modern eye.
